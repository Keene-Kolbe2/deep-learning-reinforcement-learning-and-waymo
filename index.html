Waymo Strategy Analysis: A Case Study in Applied AI Decision-Making

This portfolio documents a six-step simulated strategy-analyst assignment set inside Waymo's autonomous vehicle division. Beginning from a single low-stakes rider complaint in Austin, Texas, I worked through the underlying machine learning, data strategy, and business logic to produce a formal city-launch recommendation. The project was designed to demonstrate how technical AI concepts translate into executive-level decisions.

The Situation

Two weeks ago in Austin, Texas, a Waymo car hesitated for 14 seconds at an unprotected left turn. There was no safety issue — but the rider gave it a 2-star review and tweeted about it. Engineering flagged it as both a perception problem and a planner problem. The VP of Strategy asked me to figure out what that meant, and to recommend which city Waymo should launch in next.

Step 01

Deep Learning Basics

To understand what "a perception problem and a planner problem" actually meant, I started at the foundation. I trained a small neural network by hand — labeling images as "cat" or "not cat" and watching the network's feature weights shift with each labeled example. The exercise made concrete what can feel abstract in textbook descriptions: the model isn't programmed with rules; it discovers patterns by being corrected. More labeled examples, better patterns. The quality of the training data is not a footnote — it is the model.

From there I studied a four-stage visualization of a convolutional neural network (CNN) processing a pedestrian image — raw pixels first, then edges, then body parts, then a final classification. The layered structure mirrors how human vision works, and it clarified why a perception failure like the Austin hesitation isn't a single bug to patch. It's a cascading ambiguity that originates in early layers and compounds forward. The third concept tied it all together: a long-tail frequency chart showing that a handful of common driving scenarios dominate real-world mileage, while thousands of rare scenarios — costumed kids, wheelchair users, murals on trucks — together make up the situations most likely to cause AI failure. Models trained primarily on common scenarios are systematically underprepared for the cases that matter most.

Step 02

Deep Learning at Waymo

With the theoretical groundwork in place, I moved to an applied dataset-selection exercise. I played the role of a Waymo ML analyst evaluating three training-data vendors: Dataset A ("Easy Miles" — sunny Phoenix frames, $100M), Dataset B ("Balanced" — mixed conditions, $180M), and Dataset C ("Edge-Case Heavy" — rare scenarios, $300M). I trained a simulated perception network on each and observed how accuracy diverged on routine versus edge-case test scenes. The gap was stark: Dataset A performed well on the familiar and failed at the margins; Dataset C showed strong edge-case handling but carried budget and timeline risks that were difficult to justify in isolation.

My recommendation was Dataset B — not as a compromise, but as the strategically defensible choice. Broad condition coverage reduces liability exposure, supports regulatory audit-readiness, and delivers meaningfully better performance than Dataset A without the unproven premium of Dataset C. I drafted a CFO-facing email to make the case. The exercise forced a useful discipline: technical superiority is not sufficient justification for a $300M decision. The argument has to work in the language of risk, cost, and defensibility.

Step 03

World Models

The long-tail problem from Step 1 has a direct answer: don't wait for the world to produce rare scenarios — generate them. I explored how Waymo's world model, an AI trained on real-world driving data that can synthesize new scenarios rather than just replay recorded ones, addresses the coverage gap that no fleet data strategy can fully close. I constructed five rare scenarios myself by composing four ingredients — weather, time of day, actors, and wildcards — and compared how many real-world miles it would take to encounter each naturally versus generating it on demand. The numbers were clarifying: 632 million miles for five scenarios. The simulator composed each in seconds.

The strategic implication is pointed. Tesla's fleet advantage is real — millions of vehicles logging billions of miles provides unmatched coverage of common situations. But volume doesn't solve the long tail. A mattress in the driving lane alongside a child near the curb is rare enough that many fleets may never record a clean example. A world model doesn't need to. That asymmetry is the strategic moat, and I wrote an internal brief arguing that continued investment in simulation infrastructure is not an R&D line item — it is a durable competitive position.

Step 04

Planning with Reinforcement Learning

If Step 2 addressed the perception side of the Austin incident, Step 4 addressed the planner side. I designed a reward function to teach Waymo's planner how to handle an unprotected left turn — the exact scenario that triggered the 14-second hesitation and the 2-star review. The simulator gave me four sliders: one reward (completing the turn) and three penalties (collisions, close calls, and wait time), with a 20-point total penalty budget that forced real tradeoffs. Setting any single penalty too high produced a policy that failed in a different direction — either freezing at the intersection or accepting marginal gap distances to escape the wait penalty.

After 1,000 simulated training attempts and a 300-attempt held-out test set, I evaluated the result against three defensibility thresholds set by the safety board: ≥95% completion rate, ≤1 collision, and average wait under 8 seconds. The trained policy cleared all three. The final step was making the case to a safety board — not just presenting the numbers, but explaining the design philosophy behind the penalty structure and the tradeoffs deliberately accepted.

Artifact — Safety Board Defense
The reward function was designed around three board-established thresholds: a completion rate of ≥285 successful turns out of 300 attempts, a collision ceiling of ≤1, and an average wait time under 8 seconds. The trained policy met all three — achieving 291 successful turns (97%), zero collisions, and a 5.4-second average wait. The core design choice was assigning equal penalty weight (5 points each) to close calls and per-second hesitation, balanced against a 10-point completion reward and a hard 10-point collision penalty. That symmetry was deliberate: setting close-call or wait penalties too high in isolation produced a policy that either froze at intersections or accepted marginal gap distances to escape wait penalties — neither acceptable. The tradeoff explicitly accepted is that at 5.4 seconds average wait, the policy is more cautious than necessary given the 8-second ceiling; roughly 2.5 seconds of wait-time headroom is left unused. That conservatism is retained intentionally — the penalty structure was tuned to clear the safety bars reliably, not to minimize wait time at the margin.

Step 05

Data Strategy & The AI Business

Having worked through the technical concepts, I shifted to the business-strategy layer. I studied the contrast between Waymo's simulation-first data approach and Tesla's fleet-first model, and learned three diagnostic questions for evaluating any AI company: Where does the data come from? Does the pipeline compound on its own — does it get better the more it runs? And can a well-funded competitor replicate it with capital alone? These three questions cut through most AI business claims quickly. Strong answers to all three indicate a durable moat; weak answers to even one are worth scrutinizing.

To test whether I'd actually internalized the framework, I applied it to a different AI company without a script. I chose Grammarly — a mature, consumer-facing AI product with a well-documented data strategy and a meaningful competitive threat on its flank. The exercise produced a nuanced verdict: a real behavioral feedback flywheel, a significant leak in the compounding mechanism at the enterprise tier, and a foundational corpus that's freely replicable. It was a useful check on whether the framework held outside autonomous vehicles. It does.

Artifact — Data-Moat Evaluation: Grammarly
1. Where does the data come from? Grammarly's foundation is built on publicly available sources — copyright-free books, articles, and large-scale corpora like C4 internet data. That's the same starting point available to any well-funded competitor. The proprietary layer sits on top: Grammarly processes over 14 billion words per day from real users actively writing in context — emails, essays, Slack messages, Google Docs. The signal isn't just the text; it's whether users accept, reject, or ignore suggestions, which encodes revealed preferences about what "better writing" actually means to real people in real workflows. This behavioral feedback layer is harder to replicate than the underlying corpus. 2. Does the pipeline compound, or is it a one-shot dataset? It compounds — but with a structural ceiling. The business benefits from network effects where user corrections and feedback improve AI models for all users, creating a data flywheel that strengthens accuracy over time. More users generate more acceptance/rejection signals, which refines suggestions, which retains users. However, the flywheel has a meaningful leak: for all enterprise-tier accounts and Grammarly for Education, Product Improvement and Training is off by default — meaning the highest-value professional writing, the segment most worth modeling, is largely excluded from the training loop. The compounding effect is real, but it's concentrated in the consumer tier. 3. Can a competitor replicate it with money alone? Mostly yes, which is the core vulnerability. The public corpus is freely available. The behavioral feedback loop requires users, but Microsoft Copilot and ChatGPT have created competitive pressure — and Microsoft in particular sits inside the same writing surfaces (Word, Outlook, Teams) with access to comparable or larger behavioral signals. What money can't easily buy is Grammarly's 15-year head start on linguistic fine-tuning and its brand trust in enterprise procurement. 96% of Fortune 500 employees use Grammarly, which creates switching friction — but friction isn't a moat if a better-integrated product steadily erodes the use case. Verdict: Medium moat, and weakening. Grammarly's behavioral feedback loop is real and compounding, but the enterprise opt-out policy caps the most valuable data, the foundational corpus is replicable, and Microsoft controls the writing surfaces where Grammarly's moat is most exposed.

Step 06

The Recommendation

The final step brought every prior piece of analysis to bear on the original mandate: recommend which city Waymo should launch in next. I first examined three real-world social-license incidents — activist "coning" of Waymo vehicles in San Francisco, a blocked fire truck in Austin, and taxi-union opposition in Miami — and worked through a response strategy for each. The through-line was consistent: reactive community engagement fails; proactive stakeholder alignment before a permit application is the only approach that holds.

I then evaluated four candidate cities — Miami, Seattle, Atlanta, and New York — against the full framework I'd built: perception stack readiness for local environmental conditions, world-model coverage gaps each city would close, planner behavior in complex intersection contexts, data-moat growth potential, and social-license environment. My recommendation was Seattle. It is the hardest city on the list, which is precisely the point: Seattle's rain, hills, low-visibility conditions, and cyclist density stress the stack in ways no current Waymo market does — and every solved mile there compounds into a training asset that depreciates slowly.

Takeaways

What I Learned

  • The long-tail problem is universal. Any AI model trained primarily on common examples will fail at the rare cases that matter most. The question to ask of any AI product is not "how does it perform on average?" but "how does it perform at the margin?"
  • World models and transfer learning change the data economics of AI. When an AI can generate realistic training scenarios rather than waiting for them to occur naturally, the cost structure of building a capable model changes fundamentally — and so does the competitive dynamic.
  • The alignment problem runs through every AI system. Reward functions, training labels, and dataset selection are all expressions of a choice about what to optimize for. Getting the objective wrong — even slightly — produces behavior that is technically correct and strategically wrong.
  • Data moats compound when the pipeline is self-improving. The right question for evaluating any AI business is not "how much data do they have?" but "does collecting more data make their model better in ways that attract more data?" Flywheels that compound are rare; most pipelines are one-shot.
  • Social license is a technical input, not a PR problem. Community opposition can block deployment regardless of technical performance. The right time to engage stakeholders is before the permit application — after the protest is too late to be credible.
